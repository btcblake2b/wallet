What bitcoin is — and why it matters

Before keys, fees and channels, one idea: money is a record of who owns what. Bitcoin is the first version of that record that nobody owns.

1. Money is a ledger

Open your banking app and look at the number. There is no coin anywhere with your name on it: there is a line in a database that says how much the bank owes you. When you pay someone, the bank subtracts a number from your line and adds it to theirs.

That database is called a ledger: the list of who owns what. Money has worked like this for centuries — from clay tablets to printed notes backed by an institution — and it works because everyone accepts the same ledger and trusts the same keeper.

Bitcoin is a different arrangement:

  • the ledger is public: anyone can download it and check every line;
  • it is append-only: you can add a page, you cannot rewrite the past;
  • it is kept by thousands of independent computers (nodes) that all enforce the same rules;
  • nobody can add a line in your name without a signature that only you can produce.

That is the whole invention. Not "digital money" — digital money already existed — but a ledger that no single company, bank or government can edit alone.

2. Why a ledger without a boss is useful

Most of the time your bank works fine. The interesting part is what becomes possible when nobody needs permission:

  • You can be paid by anyone, anywhere, without an account approval, a contract or a business relationship. The recipient just needs an address.
  • Nobody can freeze or reverse your payment. Great if you are a dissident, an expat sending money home, or a merchant with a competitor who likes to complain to payment processors. Also sobering: the same applies to mistakes.
  • The supply is rule-based, not political. Bitcoin's issuance is fixed by code and cannot be expanded by a central bank decision. (On this app's network, see §4.)
  • Anyone can verify. You do not have to trust an audit: you can check the ledger and the software yourself, and the source is open.
  • It works 24/7, across borders, with no closing time and no "the transfer will arrive in 3 business days".

This is usually summarised as: no permission, no counterparty, no censorship. In practice it means the ledger keeps working even if the people who built it disappear — a property no company-controlled database has.

3. What it costs you

A course that only lists advantages is advertising. Here is the bill:

  • Responsibility. There is no support desk and no chargeback. If you sign a payment to the wrong address, it is gone.
  • Backups are your job. Lose the recovery phrase and the money is unrecoverable — by anyone.
  • It is not anonymous. The ledger is public; privacy has to be earned deliberately (module 4).
  • Fees and waiting times exist. Cheap and instant is not guaranteed; it depends on how busy the network is (module 2).
  • Learning curve. You are reading a course for a reason: a wallet is closer to a safe than to a bank app.
  • Volatility and youth. Value moves, tooling changes, and young networks break. Never put in money you cannot afford to lose.

4. The network you are using: bitcoin-blake2b

This wallet does not talk to Bitcoin. It talks to bitcoin-blake2b: a separate network that started from Bitcoin's design and then took its own path.

What that means concretely:

  • Separate chain, separate coins. Balances, addresses and transactions here have nothing to do with Bitcoin's. Nothing moves between the two.
  • Different hashing (BLAKE2b). The network uses a different function to stamp blocks. Keys and address formats remain the familiar family you may have seen on Bitcoin (bc1…).
  • No consensus-level replay protection. The app signs with SIGHASH_UNIFIED to reduce the risk of a transaction being replayed on another chain, but that is a mitigation, not a guarantee: always verify network and addresses.
  • Experimental. Liquidity, exchanges and tooling are young. Treat it as a network to learn and test on, not as the place for your life savings.
  • No market price. That is why the app never shows a fiat equivalent: it would be inventing a number.
Read this twice — every rule in this course applies to bitcoin-blake2b, and none of it implies that the coins are bitcoin. If a tutorial, video or "support agent" mixes the two, stop reading it.

5. Why a wallet and not an account

With an exchange you get a login: the company holds the keys and shows you a number. You own a promise.

With a wallet you get a key: the number on the screen is derived from the chain, and only your key can move it. You own the thing itself.

That is why a wallet never asks you to sign up, never knows your name, and cannot reset your password. It is also why the first thing a wallet asks you to do is write down a backup (module 3) — it is the only thing standing between you and a lost balance.

Takeaway — Bitcoin is a public ledger that no one owns, where ownership is proven by a key instead of an institution. Freedom from permission is the product; responsibility, irreversibility and learning are the price. The network this app uses is a separate, experimental chain: same ideas, different coins.
